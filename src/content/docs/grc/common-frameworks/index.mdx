Security frameworks proliferate, and the instinct is to treat them as interchangeable checklists
of controls. They're not — they differ in *kind*, and understanding what each is *for* is what
lets you avoid [implementing the same control seven times](/grc/compliance-mapping/) for seven
audits. The useful distinction is the role each plays, not the controls each contains.

:::note[A landscape that moves]
Framework versions, control counts, and certification schemes change. This section is about the
durable *shape* of each framework and how they relate; verify current versions and specifics
before citing them in a real assessment.
:::

## Frameworks differ by kind

Sort them by what they actually are and most of the confusion clears:

- **Voluntary frameworks** you adopt to structure a program — [NIST CSF](/grc/common-frameworks/nist-csf/),
  [CIS Controls](/grc/common-frameworks/cis-controls/).
- **Certifiable standards** a third party audits you against —
  [ISO 27001](/grc/common-frameworks/iso-27001/), [SOC 2](/grc/common-frameworks/soc-2/).
- **Mandatory regimes** required to operate in a market or sector —
  [FedRAMP](/grc/common-frameworks/fedramp/), [PCI DSS](/grc/common-frameworks/pci-dss/).
- **Control catalogs** you draw from rather than certify against —
  [NIST SP 800-53](/grc/common-frameworks/nist-800-53/).

A framework's kind determines how you use it. You *adopt* CSF, *get certified* against ISO,
*achieve authorization* under FedRAMP, and *select from* 800-53. Treating a catalog as a
checklist, or a voluntary framework as a certification, is a category error that wastes effort.

## The major frameworks

Each framework below has its own page — what it is, the distinction that trips people up, and
where it connects to the rest of the site. They are grouped by the kind above.

| Framework | Kind | The distinction that matters |
|---|---|---|
| [NIST CSF 2.0](/grc/common-frameworks/nist-csf/) | Voluntary | An organizing structure, not a control list — CSF 2.0 made Govern its own function |
| [CIS Controls & Benchmarks](/grc/common-frameworks/cis-controls/) | Voluntary | The one that tells you the actual setting, which is why it doubles as a posture baseline |
| [ISO/IEC 27001 & 27002](/grc/common-frameworks/iso-27001/) | Certifiable | 27001 certifies the management *system*; 27002 is the control guidance |
| [SOC 2](/grc/common-frameworks/soc-2/) | Certifiable | Type I attests design, Type II attests operation — Type II is the one customers want |
| [FedRAMP](/grc/common-frameworks/fedramp/) | Mandatory | Authorization to sell cloud to the US government, built on 800-53 baselines |
| [PCI DSS v4.x](/grc/common-frameworks/pci-dss/) | Mandatory | Enforced by the card brands, not a government; narrow scope, deep within it |
| [NIST SP 800-53](/grc/common-frameworks/nist-800-53/) | Catalog | The common denominator most other frameworks quietly map back to |
| [HITRUST CSF](/grc/common-frameworks/hitrust/) | Certifiable | Bundles and maps other frameworks into one certifiable, healthcare-oriented program |
| [CMMC](/grc/common-frameworks/cmmc/) | Mandatory | The US defense supply chain's certification of NIST 800-171 |

## What they share, and why that matters

Strip the vocabulary differences and the major frameworks converge on the same control families:
access control, change management, logging and monitoring, incident response, vulnerability
management, encryption, and vendor risk. **They are largely the same controls in different
language**, which is the entire foundation of the [common-control approach](/grc/compliance-mapping/)
— implement the control once, evidence it against many frameworks.

That convergence is also why the controls trace to the other pillars directly: access control is
[IAM](/iam/) governance, secure change management is [AppSec SDLC](/appsec/sdlc/),
logging and monitoring is [detection](/detection-eng/program-metrics/), and encryption is
[key management](/cloud-infra/cryptography-key-management/). A framework requirement is
rarely a new thing to build; it's usually a demand for evidence that something you should already
be doing is being done and can be shown.

## Choosing, without collecting them all

The mistake is pursuing frameworks for their own sake. The driver should be external: customers
demand SOC 2, the government market demands FedRAMP, payment processing demands PCI, the sector
demands HITRUST. Choose CSF or CIS as your internal organizing spine, add certifications as the
market requires them, and lean on [mapping](/grc/compliance-mapping/) so each addition is
incremental rather than a fresh program. A team chasing certifications nobody asked for is
spending security budget on paperwork.

## Where this connects

Frameworks are the source of the control requirements the rest of the site *implements* — this
pillar is where [IAM](/iam/), [AppSec](/appsec/sdlc/), [detection](/detection-eng/),
and [cloud](/cloud-infra/) get their obligations. The mechanism that stops each framework
being separate work is [compliance mapping](/grc/compliance-mapping/), and turning the whole thing
from paperwork into pipeline is [GRC engineering](/grc/grc-engineering/).
