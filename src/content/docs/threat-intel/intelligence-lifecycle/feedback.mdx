[The parent page](/threat-intel/intelligence-lifecycle/) argues that feedback is the phase
separating programs from report factories. This page is the mechanics, and the mechanics
matter because of one property: **feedback almost never arrives on its own.** Consumers
who acted on a report see no reason to say so; consumers who ignored it have even less.
A team that waits for feedback will conclude from silence whatever it prefers to believe.
The phase has to be instrumented.

## Four signals, ranked by strength

1. **Actions observed.** A [backlog entry](/detection-eng/detection-lifecycle/) citing the
   report, a risk register update, a patch window moved, a decision memo quoting the
   assessment. Strongest because it is revealed preference — nobody performs a Jira ticket
   to be polite.
2. **Explicit statements.** The consumer says what they did or decided. Strong, with one
   systematic bias: politeness. "Useful, thanks" is noise; the calibrating question is
   *what did you do differently?* — and "nothing, but keep sending it" is a real answer
   that should be recorded as the negative signal it is.
3. **Usage telemetry.** Opens, clicks, portal views. Weak: it measures circulation, and a
   skim is indistinguishable from a decision. Useful mainly at the extremes — a product
   nobody even opens is answering a question nobody asked.
4. **Silence.** The default, and worth exactly nothing in either direction — yet it is
   what most recurring products run on for years. Treat unmeasured products as unread
   until evidence says otherwise.

## Mechanisms: make the asking cheap

Each mechanism exists to convert silence into one of the stronger signals at near-zero
cost to the consumer:

- **Attach the question to delivery.** One line at the foot of the product: *did this
  change anything — one sentence back.* Response rates are low; the responses are gold.
- **Take a standing agenda slot** in the consumer team's own review meeting — five minutes
  in *their* ritual outperforms a survey in yours every time.
- **Close RFIs with the requester.** An [RFI](/threat-intel/intelligence-requirements/)
  is finished when the requester confirms the question is answered — which captures the
  action while it is still fresh. Repeat requesters are the clearest loyalty signal a
  program gets.
- **Tag products with the requirement they serve.** The tag tells the consumer why they
  received the thing, and it routes whatever feedback returns onto the requirement —
  where the [quarterly review](/threat-intel/intelligence-requirements/) will actually
  use it.

## Detection outcomes are feedback with the politeness removed

For tactical products, the truthful consumer is the detection. Intelligence that became
[rules](/detection-eng/detection-lifecycle/) generates outcome data with no social filter:
true-positive yield says the intelligence described something real in your environment; a
[false-positive stream](/detection-eng/detection-quality/) traced to intel-derived content
says the source that supplied it deserves scrutiny. Wiring rule outcomes back to the feed,
report, or community that spawned the rule turns the SOC's daily grind into a continuous
quality score on collection — feedback nobody had to be asked for.

## The product portfolio: pilot, cadence, review, retire

Recurring products deserve the same lifecycle discipline as
[requirements](/threat-intel/intelligence-requirements/), because they rot the same way —
the consumer changes roles, the decision disappears, the format fossilizes:

- **Pilot** with a named consumer and a stated question before anything becomes "weekly."
- **Cadence** is a choice to revisit — a weekly nobody acts on may be a monthly, or a
  requirement-triggered alert.
- **Review** each product on the requirements cadence, against the signals above rather
  than recollection.
- **Retire** without ceremony. A retired product is capacity returned to questions someone
  is asking. The quiet test works: pause a suspect product and count the days before
  anyone notices. A month of nobody asking *is* the review.

Zombie products carry a cost beyond the hours they consume: they train consumers that
material from the intel team is skippable, and that expectation transfers to the products
that matter.

## The boundary with program metrics

This page is the phase run per product and per consumer.
[Program metrics](/threat-intel/running-a-cti-program/) aggregate these signals to defend
the program's existence and budget — the same numbers at portfolio altitude, the mirror of
[how detection engineering reports itself](/detection-eng/program-metrics/). The division
of labour: feedback decides *what to fix*; metrics prove *what it was worth*. And
feedback's terminal output flows to direction — every signal collected here is an input to
the next [requirements review](/threat-intel/intelligence-requirements/), which is what
"closing the loop" means concretely.

## Where this connects

The signals feed [the requirements review](/threat-intel/intelligence-requirements/) and
aggregate into [program metrics](/threat-intel/running-a-cti-program/). Detection outcomes
arrive via [the detection lifecycle](/detection-eng/detection-lifecycle/) and
[detection quality](/detection-eng/detection-quality/), making the SOC the one consumer
whose feedback is automatic. The phase this page instruments is the last arc of
[the loop](/threat-intel/intelligence-lifecycle/), and the reason the loop deserves the
name.
