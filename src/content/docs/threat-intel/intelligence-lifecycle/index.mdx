Every other page in this pillar covers a stage of work: collecting, analysing, writing,
briefing. This page is the loop that connects them — the intelligence cycle — and an honest
account of how it behaves once a real team tries to run it.

The model earns its place by forcing one claim: intelligence is production, with a customer
at both ends. The loop starts with someone's question, ends with an answer delivered to that
person, and continues only when they say whether it helped. Every failure mode this pillar
warns about — reports nobody reads, analysis without a "so what" — is a phase of this loop
quietly missing.

## The six phases

- **Direction.** Deciding what needs to be known, and for whom. The output is a set of
  [requirements](/threat-intel/intelligence-requirements/) with a named consumer attached.
  This is the phase most often skipped entirely, and everything downstream inherits the
  omission.
- **Collection.** Gathering against those requirements — feeds, OSINT, internal telemetry.
  The craft lives in [Collection & Sourcing](/threat-intel/collection-sourcing/); the
  lifecycle's contribution is the word *against*. Collection without a requirement is
  hoarding.
- **Processing.** Turning raw material into something analysable: parsing, normalising,
  deduplicating, enriching. Mostly engineering, chronically under-resourced, and the one
  phase no other subsection owns — covered in full in
  [Processing & Enrichment](/threat-intel/intelligence-lifecycle/processing/).
- **Analysis.** The step where observations become a judgement — the whole subject of
  [Analytic Methods](/threat-intel/analytic-methods/).
- **Dissemination.** The judgement, in a form its consumer can act on:
  [reporting](/threat-intel/report-writing/) and
  [briefing](/threat-intel/effective-communication/).
- **Feedback.** Whether it answered the question, what the consumer did, and what the
  requirement should become next. Instrumenting this phase is
  [its own page](/threat-intel/intelligence-lifecycle/feedback/).

craft elsewhere in the pillar:

## Feedback separates programs from report factories

A report factory runs the first five phases on a calendar and measures itself by output:
reports shipped, indicators pushed, briefings held. The loop never closes, so production
drifts toward whatever is easiest to produce — and the weekly digest that nobody reads can
run for years on pure momentum, because no mechanism exists for its irrelevance to register.

The feedback phase is one question asked with a straight face: *what did you do differently
because of this?* It is uncomfortable precisely because it is diagnostic. A consumer who
cannot name an action, a decision, or a changed prior is telling you the product serves no
requirement — and that finding should flow back to direction and change what gets made.
This is also the honest basis for program metrics: work produced against a requirement
counts differently from work produced because it was interesting. Which signals count,
how to collect them without begging, and when to retire a product are covered in
[Feedback & Product Health](/threat-intel/intelligence-lifecycle/feedback/).

## F3EAD, the loop at operations tempo

Targeting doctrine runs a different cycle: **find, fix, finish, exploit, analyze,
disseminate**. Where the classic cycle runs on requirements and a production calendar,
F3EAD runs on contact — the [incident response arc](/dfir/incident-response/) up front,
and behind it the **exploit** phase, which names something defenders systematically waste:
a finished incident is ground truth about your own environment, your actual adversary, and
your controls under load, richer than anything a feed will ever deliver. The two loops fit
different teams and compose — what the fast loop's exploit phase surfaces becomes an
update to the standing requirements the slow loop runs on. The full defensive
translation, phase by phase, is in [F3EAD](/threat-intel/intelligence-lifecycle/f3ead/).

## Where the neat loop breaks

The cycle is a genuinely useful model carrying some honest caveats:

- **The phases run concurrently.** An analyst mid-analysis goes back and collects. A team
  is in all six phases at once across a dozen products. Anyone auditing a team for clean
  sequential phase transitions is measuring the diagram rather than the work.
- **The model assumes an institution.** It was drawn for an apparatus with separated roles —
  tasking authorities, dedicated collectors, all-source analysts. A two-person CTI team *is*
  the whole diagram, and imposing ceremony at the phase boundaries adds cost without adding
  rigour.
- **Most programs run the loop backwards.** Collection arrives first — the feeds were
  bought, the alerts flow — and "requirements" get reverse-engineered to justify what is
  already coming in. Supply-driven intelligence answers the questions the vendor's
  visibility can answer, which overlap only by luck with the questions your organization
  needs answered.

Read as a workflow to enforce, the cycle produces process theatre. Read as a diagnostic
checklist, it earns its keep: when intelligence is being produced and nothing is changing,
"which phase is missing?" almost always has a specific answer — usually direction or
feedback, the two phases where the customer lives.

## The loop in a working week

The abstraction maps onto a calendar more directly than it first appears, because the loop
is fractal — it runs at several cadences at once:

- Morning triage is collection and processing against standing requirements.
- An ad hoc question from IR runs the entire loop in miniature, inside a day.
- The weekly product is a dissemination cadence, and the two minutes spent asking its
  readers what they used is the feedback phase.
- The incident retrospective is F3EAD's exploit phase wearing blameless-postmortem clothes.
- The quarterly requirements review is direction, done deliberately.

The test is concrete: point to where direction and feedback happen in your team's actual
calendar. If no meeting, ritual, or document does either job, those phases are aspirational —
and the loop is an assembly line with nobody ordering and nobody receiving.
